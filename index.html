01UQ

Uncertainty Quantification for Simulation Metrology

Trustworthy simulation · 17,280 model combinations
Research problem

Simulation is trusted to measure nanometre-scale features on microchips — yet the reliability of those predictions had never been quantified across the full space of physical models feeding the calculation.

Scientific motivation

Every simulated measurement rests on interchangeable choices of scattering and energy-loss models. If those choices move the answer more than the measurement tolerance, the result cannot be trusted for metrology.

Methodology

The first systematic uncertainty study of its kind: line-scan profiles were regenerated across 17,280 combinations of elastic and inelastic model inputs, isolating geometric, physical-model, and statistical error contributions.

Computational approach

Ensemble Monte-Carlo campaigns were parallelised with MPI on the USTC supercomputing cluster, with structured, version-controlled pipelines for statistical post-processing of the resulting spatial fields.

Key achievements
  • Delivered the first end-to-end uncertainty budget for simulated critical-dimension line scans.
  • Separated model-driven uncertainty from statistical noise, giving experimentalists a defensible confidence interval.
  • Established a reusable benchmarking framework now applied to further metrology and transport studies.
Publication — Khan, M.S.S. et al., “Uncertainty evaluation of Monte Carlo simulated line scan profiles of a CD-SEM,” J. Appl. Phys. 133, 245303 (2023). doi:10.1063/5.0153379 ↗
02RAD

Radiation-Tolerant Alloy Design via Defect Evolution

FeNiAl superlattices · OKMC · nuclear & fusion materials
Research problem

Structural alloys degrade as irradiation displaces atoms and breeds defect clusters. Can nanoscale microstructure be tuned so the material heals faster than it is damaged?

Scientific motivation

Radiation tolerance is the limiting factor for the lifetime of reactor components and fusion first walls. Precipitate interfaces act as sinks that trap and annihilate defects — but only at the right size and density.

Methodology

A multiscale chain links primary-damage generation to long-time defect evolution: ion transport and cascades feed cascade-annealing kinetics, which feed object kinetic Monte-Carlo evolution of the defect population.

Computational approach

Coupled IM3D → CRT → MMonCa workflow with format translation, consistency validation, and automated regression checks so physics propagates correctly from stage to stage.

Key achievements
  • Identified an optimal precipitate design — approximately 20 nm diameter at ~20% volume fraction — maximising defect recombination in FeNiAl superlattice alloys.
  • Built and self-authored a full cascade-annealing code and the coupling “bridge” codes that make the three engines interoperate.
  • Restructured cluster-scale campaigns to run tractably on workstation hardware without loss of fidelity.
Programme output — manuscripts in preparation and conference presentations at international materials-science and physics meetings. Detailed citations available on request [ ].
033D

Full-3D Geometry in Ion-Transport Monte Carlo

Gmsh meshing · superlattices · thousands of nanoparticles
Research problem

Leading transport codes assume simple, planar geometries — yet real radiation-tolerant materials are dense three-dimensional superlattices of curved nanoparticles.

Scientific motivation

Geometry fidelity directly determines simulation accuracy. Without faithful 3D structure, the interfaces that govern defect behaviour simply do not exist in the model.

Methodology

Parametric 3D geometry generation with Gmsh and Python — triangular meshing of non-planar interfaces, curved boundaries, and high feature-density domains — embedded directly into transport codes previously limited to basic shapes.

Computational approach

Parallel solvers engineered for very large geometries containing thousands of nanoparticles, a direct analogue of production mesh-to-solver interoperability at scale.

Key achievements
  • A signature contribution: enabling complex embedded geometry in codes that had only handled simple domains.
  • Reusable parametric pipeline for nanoparticle, multilayer, and superlattice targets.
  • Validated 3D representations against transport observables across the working energy range.
Methodology and geometry-scripting support (Gmsh / Python nanoparticle meshing) available for collaborative projects. Related citations [ ].
04LIB

Physics Libraries From Scratch: Cross-Sections & Energy Loss

Mott ×384 · dielectric ELF · Penn / Mermin / Levine–Louie
Research problem

Every particle-transport prediction is only as good as the elastic and inelastic physics fed into it — the cross-sections and energy-loss functions describing how particles change direction and lose energy.

Scientific motivation

Reliable, self-consistent physics libraries are the foundation of credible simulation and of the uncertainty studies built on top of them.

Methodology

Self-authored libraries: a Mott elastic-scattering suite spanning 384 cross-section models, and dielectric-response energy-loss functions implementing Penn, Mermin, and Levine–Louie treatments of inelastic scattering.

Computational approach

Numerical algorithm development in C++/Fortran with Python analysis, benchmarked by cross-code validation to establish which results are reliable.

Key achievements
  • An extensive theoretical quantification of secondary-electron emission from silicon.
  • Electron backscattering and emission-yield studies for Cr, Co, Pd, and Ge solids.
  • Libraries reused across the group’s simulation stack and downstream metrology work.
Publication — Khan, M.S.S. et al., “An extensive theoretical quantification of secondary electron emission from silicon,” Vacuum 215, 112257 (2023). doi:10.1016/j.vacuum.2023.112257 ↗

Elastic vs inelastic scattering

About

A computational scientist at the intersection of physics, materials, and AI

Dr. Muhammad Saadat Shakoor Khan develops physics-faithful simulations — and the AI methods around them — to answer a single kind of question: what happens when energetic particles strike matter?

The work began in computational physics at the University of the Punjab in Lahore and matured over a decade inside a single research group at the University of Science and Technology of China and the Chinese Academy of Sciences, from an MSc through a PhD in condensed-matter physics to postdoctoral research at the Hefei Institute of Physical Sciences. Throughout, one thread holds: large-scale Monte-Carlo simulation of particle–solid interactions, built and validated from the ground up.

That core now reaches from semiconductor metrology — where simulation supports nanometre-scale measurement of microchips — to radiation materials science, engineering nanostructured alloys that survive the punishing environments of nuclear reactors, fusion devices, and space. Alongside the physics runs a machine-learning practice: manifold learning, Gaussian-process denoising, and uncertainty quantification that make simulation faster and more trustworthy.

The distinctive signature is craft in code: simulation engines, energy-loss and cross-section libraries, cascade-annealing routines, and 3D geometry pipelines written from scratch — the kind of infrastructure that lets complex, real-world geometry pass through codes built only for simple shapes.
